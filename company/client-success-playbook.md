# TradingLab03 Client Experience: Build Exactly What Was Approved

## Promise to the client

We do not promise to read minds or deliver every idea at the first price. We promise a clear process: listen, document, confirm, build against agreed checks, show progress, test, hand over, and plan the next version together.

## 1. Discovery call / intake

Capture:
- Business goal and current problem
- Audience, location/language, and accessibility needs
- Pages/screens/deliverables for version 1
- Each required feature and what it must do
- Visual references, brand rules, and things the client dislikes
- Supplied copy, images, logos, product data, and access dependencies
- Required integrations, data flows, and permission boundaries
- Budget range, deadline, review milestones, and decision maker
- Acceptance criteria that can be tested
- Future ideas in a separate backlog, not silently included in version 1
- Desired support after launch

Never ask a client to paste passwords, private keys, API tokens, or payment details into the brief.

## 2. Turn the brief into a scope agreement

Before coding, send the client a plain-language scope containing:
1. Version number and goal.
2. In-scope pages and deliverables.
3. Feature-by-feature expected behavior.
4. Out-of-scope items and dependencies.
5. Content/assets the client must provide.
6. Acceptance checks and supported devices/browsers.
7. Milestones, review dates, and client feedback turnaround.
8. Price, payment milestones, revisions, ownership, cancellation, and support terms—approved by the owner and agreed in writing.
9. A separate version 2+ wishlist.
10. A written approval record.

**Gate:** no build starts until scope, price, deadline, dependencies, and acceptance criteria are approved in writing.

## 3. Use acceptance criteria, not vague adjectives

Translate “modern,” “fast,” “easy,” or “make it like this” into examples and checks. For each feature record:
- User action
- Expected result
- Error/empty state
- Device/browser requirements
- Evidence used to verify completion

Example: “On a phone viewport of 390 px, the menu can be opened and closed by keyboard and touch; all links work; no horizontal overflow appears.” This is an example only—agree the real criteria with the client.

## 4. Prototype and review before full build

- Show a low-cost structure/wireframe or representative first screen.
- Confirm brand colors, typography, navigation, tone, and main call to action.
- Ask for one consolidated set of feedback by an agreed date.
- Record what changed and which scope version is current.
- Do not present a concept as production-ready or use unlicensed assets.

## 5. Build in small, visible milestones

Suggested stages:
1. Approved brief and content/assets.
2. Structure/wireframe approval.
3. First working draft.
4. Feature implementation.
5. Independent QA and accessibility/security checks appropriate to risk.
6. Client acceptance testing.
7. Fix in-scope defects.
8. Handover and approved deployment.

Send a concise progress update at agreed milestones: completed, next, blockers, and decisions needed. Never invent progress or say a feature works before testing it.

## 6. Handle extra features fairly

Every new idea goes into a change request:
- Request and business reason
- Priority: must-have, should-have, or later
- Estimated impact on cost, timeline, risk, and existing work
- Dependencies and acceptance criteria
- Client approval and a new scope/version reference

Keep the original version stable. Do not surprise the client with a higher invoice or silently absorb unlimited work. Build version 1 to its agreement; maintain a visible backlog for later.

## 7. QA and client acceptance

For each agreed item:
- [ ] Pass/fail check against its acceptance criterion
- [ ] Mobile, desktop, keyboard, and major-browser review as scoped
- [ ] Links, forms, contact actions, and integrations tested safely
- [ ] Empty/error/loading states checked where relevant
- [ ] Content, spelling, metadata, and supplied assets reviewed
- [ ] Accessibility checks appropriate to the project completed
- [ ] Security and privacy review appropriate to data and integrations completed
- [ ] Known limitations and unresolved items documented
- [ ] Client has a preview and a clear feedback deadline
- [ ] Owner approves release; written client approval is recorded

Automated static checks are useful but are not a substitute for human/browser testing.

## 8. Handover that earns trust

Deliver:
- Final approved files/source and agreed access ownership
- Setup/deployment instructions and backup/rollback notes where applicable
- A list of features, known limitations, and third-party services
- Short “how to update it” instructions or walkthrough
- Credentials transferred through a secure, agreed method—not pasted into source code
- Warranty/bug-fix window and maintenance boundary
- Invoice and payment record; count revenue only when verified

Never deploy to a client domain or change production systems without explicit written approval and verified access.

## 9. Make repeat business natural, not pushy

At handover:
- Ask whether the delivered work meets the agreed checks.
- Invite one consolidated feedback list and resolve agreed in-scope defects.
- Offer an optional next-step roadmap ranked by impact, effort, and cost.
- Offer maintenance only if the client wants it, with clear scope and price.
- Set a follow-up only with permission and a specific purpose.
- Record preferences, decisions, known issues, and next agreed action.
- Respect no-reply, opt-out, or “not now.” No spam or fake urgency.

Useful repeat-service options may include monthly backups/updates, content refreshes, accessibility checks, analytics review, new landing pages, new integrations, or feature iterations—only where relevant and within our capability.

## 10. Measure client happiness honestly

After delivery, ask a short optional feedback set:
- Did the result match the approved brief?
- Which part was most useful?
- What was confusing or missing?
- Was communication and timing clear?
- May we use an anonymized or named testimonial? Obtain explicit permission before publishing.

Track on-time delivery, acceptance defects, revision causes, support response time, client satisfaction, repeat bookings, refunds, and profit after costs. Never fabricate testimonials, ratings, metrics, or case studies.
